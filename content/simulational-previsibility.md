## Slow execution

I was watching _Devs_, Alex Garland’s science-fiction miniseries, and I liked many of the ideas it wanted to explore. My problem was how long it took to explore them.

I have nothing against slow stories. What bothers me is **a story that gives me too much time and too little to think about**. Shows like _Mr. Robot_, _Succession_, or _Billions_ constantly force me to update my mental model of what is happening. One piece of information changes my interpretation of another; a line of dialogue changes the intention I attribute to a character; a possibility that seemed unlikely suddenly has to compete with three others. There isn’t much time left to guess what happens next because I’m still recalculating what just happened.

_Devs_ was having the opposite effect on me. Often, I had already finished thinking about an idea while the show was still running its presentation of it.

So I did something I normally hate: I asked for spoilers. More specifically, I asked an artificial intelligence to tell me, episode by episode, the rest of the story, starting from where I last watched, episode 2. I wanted access to the ideas in _Devs_ without having to execute all the hours required to reach them. That choice will become ironically important later, but, for now, I just wanted to know how the show ended.

## Simulation Doesn’t Bother Me

As ChatGPT walked me through the episodes, we started discussing the ideas behind them. Determinism, free will, quantum mechanics, Many-Worlds, simulation.

And I realized that the reason _Devs_ could have felt too slow for me is that one of the series' core dillemas, presumably supposed to provoke some kind of metaphysical vertigo, simply didn’t. If our universe is simulable[^1], what if it really is simulated? My reaction is just: _okay_. Not because I believe I know or hope that we live in a simulation. I have no idea, and the hypothesis could totally be completely wrong.

What I fail to see is why a simulated universe would, for that reason alone, be less of a universe. It isn’t clear to me in what relevant sense that reality loses its status merely because it exists on some external substrate.

We are already comfortable with the fact that what we experience as solid objects is, at more fundamental levels of description, something quite different from our macroscopic experience of them. The same runs for colors, computers, societies. Discovering another layer underneath does not necessarily make the layer above it false.

A simulated universe may simply be another universe. That moves the interesting question somewhere else. Instead of asking, “Are we real?” — which seems to me rather uninteresting without an extremely careful definition of _real_ — we can ask what follows if we take the simulation hypothesis seriously.

And I carried one very interesting (and maybe wrong) assumption about that hypothesis into the conversation.

## Simulation purposes

My default assumption has always been that if someone goes through the trouble of simulating a universe, then it _must be_ because something about the results, properties, structures, or information generated by that simulation is of interest to whoever is running it. That intuition came from thinking about the simulations we build ourselves.

When an engineer simulates a bridge, an airplane, or a roller coaster, they usually do it because they want to discover something: how much stress will appear at this point on the track? How will the system respond to a particular load? Is there a dangerous resonant frequency? Will some component fail? The simulation is valuable precisely because we do not already know every relevant detail of its outcome.

Of course, a civilization capable of simulating universes might have purposes we cannot even conceive of. Research. Historical reconstruction. Training. Entertainment. Or some category of purpose that does not exist in our universe at all. But these possibilities all seem to share a property: **there is some information in the behavior of the system that matters to whoever executes it**, and the simulation is run because something becomes available through its execution. 

That seems mundane **until the simulation in question is our universe.** Then perhaps the entire history of the cosmos — galaxy formation, evolution, civilizations, this sentence — is part of a computation whose behavior contains information of interest to some external reality. 

(_Small digression: it's interesting to think that, for a simulation this big, it would seem very unlikely that_ we _are the point of interest. Else, wouldn't it be much simpler to simulate something much smaller? But, even then, I could totally be missing the point._)

Yet an interesting observation arises: our computers seem only to be able to simulate worlds much simpler than even itself. A computer built in a Minecraft world may take a humongous space in the game's world, yet really only having 16 kilobytes of memory. Running Conway's Game of Life requires a complete Turing machine, and a capable one at that. Follow the line of reasoning and it seems to imply that computing an universe seems to require a substrate orders of magnitude more complex. Simulating our universe would imply a massive complexity on its underlying computer and its universe. Impossible, one could think.

But that assumes something we have no reason to assume: that our universe is computationally impressive.

## Universes Beneath Universes

Imagine, then, purely as a hypothesis, that it is _at least_ easier for one universe to simulate another universe of lower physical complexity than one of comparable complexity[^2]. 

**There is no obvious reason to imagine that we occupy the top of such a hierarchy**. Perhaps the universe containing ours has vastly more _degrees of freedom_. Perhaps it has elementary particles we do not have, a richer spatial structure, or physical resources for computation that would be inconceivable here.

To its inhabitants, our entire cosmos might be relatively simple.

This suggests a curious chain:

$$
U_0 \rightarrow U_1 \rightarrow U_2 \rightarrow U_3 \rightarrow \cdots
$$

where $U_i$, the universes simulating $U_{i+1}$, are capable of computationally implementing another, possibly less complex universe.

This is, of course, mere speculation, **not** a known consequence of physics. We do not even know whether a single metric of “universe complexity” would make sense in this way. But the hypothesis removes an unnecessary association between the apparent enormity of our cosmos and the cost it would impose on some external physics. To NPCs in _The Sims_, their worlds are complex enough that they have trouble navigating it themselves. We're barely figuring our way in Earth ourselves.

The same applies to time. There is no reason why one second in the simulating universe should correspond to one second here. Thirteen billion years of internal history could correspond to a completely different duration on the substrate executing the simulation. More, it could be, but potentially less. Linking back to [[#Simulation purposes|the purposes of simulation]], would anyone run a simulation if you couldn't let it unfold fast enough?

It was at this point that the AI made an apparently innocent observation.

Even if the simulators were extraordinarily powerful, perhaps they would still have to let the simulation run in order to discover what happens.

That sounded wrong to me.

If the universe is deterministic, why should they have to wait?

## If There Is Only One Future, Why Compute It Step by Step?

Suppose, for the moment, that the universe is perfectly deterministic.

> You may have noticed by now that I'm inheriting the series' context of determinism, but to the savvy reader, know that I'm not taking the position that this needs to be true of the real world.  

Let its state at time $t$ be $S_t$. Suppose there is some physical rule, a function $F$ of this universe's dynamics, determining the next state:

$$
S_{t+1}=F(S_t).
$$

Then it follows that:

$$
S_t=F^t(S_0).
$$

If we know $S_0$ and $F$ perfectly, there is exactly one possible value for $S_t$.

My objection was simple: if that value is unique, why do we need to compute all the intermediate states to find it?

Consider a trivial system:

$$
x_{n+1}=x_n+1.
$$

We could execute it literally:

```python
def iterate(initial_state: int, steps: int): 
	state = initial_state

	for i in range(steps):
	    state = state + 1

	return state

print(iterate(0, 1_000))  # prints "1000"
```

But we don’t have to. We can simply write:

$$
x_n=x_0+n.
$$

You don't need to count to a thousand to know it is the number that comes a thousand units after 0. A billion transitions have been replaced by a direct evaluation.

The dynamics remain deterministic in both cases. What changed was our ability to find a **computational shortcut** through their evolution. So the interesting question was no longer whether the future was determined, it was this:

**Does every deterministic system get a computational shortcut?**

Or even:

**Is every deterministic recursive function reducible to a deterministic non-iterative function?**

This was the point at which, without immediately realizing it, our conversation stopped being primarily about metaphysics and entered theoretical computer science.

## The Great Compiler

We can make the question a little more precise.

Imagine a program that simulates a universe:

```python
state = initial_state

for t in range(target_time):
    state = physics(state)

return state
```

Now imagine an extraordinarily powerful compiler.

We give the written program `physics`, `initial_state`, and `target_time` parameters, and ask:

> Don’t execute all these steps. Produce the fastest* program that takes the same inputs and gives me the final state, same as if it ran through iterative simulation, but directly.

For some dynamics, this is perfectly possible. The fitting example $x_{n+1}=x_n+1$ is embarrassingly simple, but more interesting systems also admit shortcuts: recurrences can have closed forms; repeated operations can sometimes be accelerated through techniques such as exponentiation by squaring; linear systems can be attacked with algebraic tools that avoid naively reproducing every intermediate step. **But none of that demonstrates the existence of a general transformation capable of doing the same thing for every computation.**

We can formulate the problem this way. Given a dynamics $F$, we want some algorithm $G$ such that

$$
G(S_0,t)=F^t(S_0),
$$

but whose time complexity is substantially lower than simply applying $F$, $t$ times.

The mathematical existence of $F^t(S_0)$ is not the issue. In a deterministic system, that output is unique by construction. The question is about the **cost of discovering it**.

**A determined future is not necessarily an efficiently computable future.**

That distinction seems small, but, for me, it changed the entire conversation.

## Determinism Is Not Predictability

There is an almost natural temptation to think, still on deterministic worlds, that if we knew the laws of physics perfectly, along with the state of everything in the universe, then we would also know its future. Laplace’s demon is the extreme version of this intuition: an intelligence that knew all relevant forces and positions could calculate both past and future. But between “the future is a unique consequence of the present” and “the future can be efficiently obtained from the present” lies a computational abyss.

Determinism is a claim about the relationship between states. Predictability is a claim about the knowledge available to an observer. Computational complexity introduces a third question: How many resources are required to produce that knowledge?

It seems we can have determinism without efficient predictability. That creates a fascinating possibility: **a universe may contain no fundamental indeterminacy whatsoever and still produce genuine surprise for every observer within it**. ***Perhaps even for whoever is simulating it.***

There would be no ontological novelty — the next state would already be completely determined by the previous one — but there could still be epistemic novelty. The result would still have to be _discovered_.

This is where the concept of **computational irreducibility** entered our conversation.

The term is strongly associated with Stephen Wolfram’s work and captures, roughly speaking, the idea that for certain processes there may be no substantially shorter way of determining their behavior than allowing the computation itself to unfold.

But an important brake needs to be applied here.

There is no universal “theorem of computational irreducibility” proving that every sufficiently complicated universe must be executed step by step. Wolfram’s broad formulation is a much more ambitious claim than that.

There are, however, formal results in theoretical computer science establishing different limits on what programs can decide, predict, or optimize in general. The Halting Problem shows that there is no algorithm capable of determining, for every program and every input, whether that execution will terminate. Rice’s theorem generalizes related limitations to nontrivial semantic properties of programs. Complexity hierarchies show that additional computational resources genuinely expand the classes of problems we can solve under particular constraints. P-completeness appears in problems whose efficient parallel prediction is believed to be especially difficult. Speedup results introduce still further subtleties into the very notion of an “optimal algorithm.”

None of these results, individually, proves our cosmological hypothesis. But together they destroy a naive inference:

$$
\text{there is a unique answer} \quad\not\Rightarrow\quad \text{there is a universal shortcut for finding it}.
$$

That was exactly what I had been assuming without noticing.

## The Smallest Possible Universe

Then another problem appeared: I had been talking about finding the “smallest program” capable of calculating the evolution of the universe. **But smallest in what sense?**

Imagine a function with a finite, though unimaginably large, set of possible inputs. One strategy would be to store every answer in a (hash-)table:

$$
\text{input} \mapsto \text{output}.
$$

The description would be monstrous, but a lookup could be extremely fast. Another strategy might be a ten-line program that computes the same answer from fundamental rules but requires trillions of operations. Which is better?

There is no answer until we specify which resource we want to minimize:
- Description length is not execution time.
- Execution time is not memory usage.

One solution may trade one resource for another.

**Descriptive complexity is not computational complexity.**

That made the cosmological analogy even more interesting: perhaps the fundamental laws of our universe are an extraordinarily compact description of its dynamics. **Maybe even the smallest**. A small set of rules may, in principle, specify a colossal amount of behavior, but a compact description does not have to be a fast method of prediction.

The laws may be the most elegant program we have for describing the universe without being a lookup table that instantly tells us what will happen ten billion years from now. Perhaps knowing the rules perfectly is still not the same as knowing the outcome.

Perhaps something still has to be executed.

## What If the Simulators Have to Watch Too?

So we returned to our hypothetical creators. Suppose they exist and that they know our laws exactly (as they, we can assume, might have been its creators).

Suppose their computational resources are so far beyond ours that the entire history of humanity can be executed in a fraction of a second of their time. Even then, one distinction remains.

**Executing something extremely quickly is not the same as not having to execute it.**

If some aspects of our universe’s evolution are computationally irreducible, the simulators might accelerate the computation, parallelize whatever can be parallelized, discover domain-specific shortcuts, and possess a computational physics unimaginably superior to ours, and yet there might still be an intrinsic, minimum cost to discovering what happens.

In that scenario, simulation would not be an inefficient way of discovering the future. Rather, it would be the very process through which the future becomes known. And that produces a curious image: perhaps the beings who created our universe would not be omniscient about what they created.

Maybe our gods, too, would be watching.

## How Far Can We Walk?

At this point, the conversation began changing again, because all of these ideas are interesting, but an uncomfortable question hangs over them.

How would we know? Perhaps we live in a simulation. Perhaps we don’t.

Perhaps Many-Worlds is the best interpretation of quantum mechanics. Perhaps it isn’t.

Perhaps there is a fundamental reality. Perhaps there is an endless sequence of substrates beneath substrates. Perhaps “what is underneath?” is itself a malformed question.

More importantly, perhaps some of these possibilities produce exactly the same observations for any creature capable of existing inside our universe, and in that case, there could be a true answer about the ontology of reality while that answer remains permanently inaccessible to us.

That possibility often produces a reaction that has always bothered me: if we may not be able to answer it, why think about it? But perhaps that, too, confuses two different things.

**Being unable to cross a boundary does not make it useless to discover where that boundary lies.**

In trying to reach the ultimate ontology of the universe, we might discover that two apparently different hypotheses are experimentally equivalent. We might find internal contradictions. We might uncover assumptions we were carrying without realizing it. We might formalize vague questions until they become scientific ones. We might prove that certain answers are undecidable under particular conditions. We might discover that what appeared impossible was merely a badly formulated question.

And sometimes what one generation calls a metaphysical limit becomes the next generation’s experimental problem: **A question does not need to be solvable to be intellectually fertile.**

Perhaps there is a wall, but I at least want to walk up to it.

## The Universe Looking at Itself

There is something almost absurd about our ability to do this: for billions of years, matter did what matter does. Particles interacted. Stars were born and died. Heavy elements were produced. Planets formed. On at least one of them, chemistry became biology, and biology eventually produced systems capable of constructing models of the environment around them.

At some point, those models stopped representing only predators, food, weather, and territory. They began representing stars, then atoms, then space and time, and eventually, the very set of rules that had produced the organism constructing the model. **A piece of the universe began constructing models of the universe.** We do not need to be the only creatures capable of this for it to be extraordinary. I do hope, in fact, that we aren’t.

But there is something profoundly strange and beautiful about a reality that, at some point in its evolution, produces structures capable of asking what reality itself is. It could be that our categories are wrong. It could even be that time, causality, identity, and existence are merely cognitive approximations produced by brains that evolved to solve much more modest problems.

Perhaps we will never know. But even discovering exactly **why** we may never know would itself be a discovery.

At that point, I thought our conversation had reached its conclusion. I was wrong.

## The Computation Began Investigating the Computation

It was only near the end of the conversation that I realized we had come full circle.

Actually, twice.

The first loop was inside the argument itself.

For much of the night, our question had been computational: if a system is deterministic and its final state is already uniquely determined by its initial state, what is the value of all the intermediate states? Why find $S_1,S_2,\ldots,S_{n-1}$ if all we want to know is $S_n$?

Our entire discussion of computational irreducibility emerged from that question. That made me think that, in some cases, those intermediate states are not an inconvenience that a sufficiently intelligent observer will eventually learn to eliminate. At least in this case, execution was precisely the process required to make the result accessible.

Hours later, after computation had turned into metaphysics, we asked the same question without noticing: if we may never reach the ultimate ontology of the universe, why keep trying? If there is an epistemic wall we can never cross, why walk toward it?

The structure was identical. In one case, we were asking why we should execute a computation if all we care about is its result. In the other, we were asking why we should execute an investigation if we may never reach its final answer. And the answer we found had the same form:

**Perhaps the value of a process does not lie only in the final state it produces.**

The search for fundamental reality may never deliver fundamental reality to us. But during its execution, it produces mathematics, physics, philosophy, instruments, experiments, new questions, and even proofs about what we cannot know.

The path is not merely the price we pay for not yet having the answer. Rather, it produces things that did not exist when the question was asked.

That was the first loop. The second was happening to the article itself.

## The Shortcut That Produced the Journey

I started that night looking for a shortcut.

Literally.

I didn’t have the patience to finish _Devs_. I was at episode two and wanted to know the final state without executing all the intermediate ones.

If we treated the episodes as an absurdly simplified dynamics, I didn’t want to step through every episode:

$$
S_2\rightarrow S_3\rightarrow S_4\rightarrow S_5\rightarrow S_6\rightarrow S_7\rightarrow S_8.
$$

I wanted some function:

$$
G(S_2,8)=S_8.
$$

And that is exactly what I asked an artificial intelligence to give me, and the funny thing, contextually, is that it worked: I found out how _Devs_ ended without watching the rest of _Devs_. My little optimization problem was solved.

**Except the result that made the conversation memorable was not $S_8$.**

As the AI described the episodes, I began objecting to some of the show’s ideas. That led us to simulation. My assumption about why someone would execute a simulation led to the question of whether the simulator would have to wait. My resistance to that answer led us to determinism. Determinism led us to computational complexity. Complexity forced us to separate description from execution, mathematical existence from computability, and computability from efficiency. And the limits of prediction eventually led us to the limits of knowledge.

None of those questions were contained in the result I had requested, they merely appeared during the execution.

And this is where the second symmetry closes the first. The content of this article argues that perhaps we cannot reduce the entire value of a computation to its final state. The existence of this article is a consequence of our not doing exactly that.

Had that conversation been perfectly optimized for my original intention, it would have been very short:

“Tell me what happens after episode two.” The AI would tell me, I would say thanks (because of course I would), and this would be the end of the computation. We would have reached the requested result more efficiently, and this article would not exist.

**The argument of the article ended up being enacted by the process that produced the article itself.**

I do not want to turn a narrative coincidence into a mathematical proof. It proves nothing about computational irreducibility, says nothing about whether we live in a simulation, and certainly does not establish an ontology for the universe.

It does something I find much more delightful. **It makes the form of the article rhyme with its content.**

We began with a computation I wanted to avoid, and ended up asking whether certain computations can be avoided. We discovered that some results may only become accessible through execution, then carried the same structure into our attempt to understand reality and asked whether an investigation is still worthwhile when its final result may be inaccessible. **And finally, we realized that the investigation that produced that conclusion existed only because we did not follow the shortest path toward the answer we originally wanted.** The execution of that investigation produced something that was not part of its original objective. Perhaps, after all, the point of _this_ simulation was the simulation itself.

Perhaps we will never know whether our universe is fundamental. Perhaps it is a simulation. Perhaps it belongs to a hierarchy of realities we will never have the means to observe. Perhaps some of these questions are, in principle, impossible to answer. But I still want to walk up to the wall, now with an additional reason.

Perhaps what we find along the way will not merely be an imperfect approximation of the answer we were looking for. Perhaps we will find questions that do not exist yet. That is exactly what happened here:

I started out wanting to know how a story ended, and ended up discovering a question I did not know I wanted to ask.

And then I realized that the text born from that question had the same structure as the answer the question had led us toward.

**Perhaps, sometimes, the most interesting result of a computation is what it produces while trying to reach the result.**
